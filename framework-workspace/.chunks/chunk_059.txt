It isn’t perfect in the strongest sense. It is perfect only in a **relative, derivative, limited sense**.

The word “perfect” is being used in two different ways:

1. **Absolute perfection** — no lack, no parts, no change, no dependence, timeless, complete, self-existent.

2. **Relative perfection** — fully satisfies a standard, purpose, or form. It lacks nothing _as a manuscript_, but it still has parts, depends on paper and ink, exists in time, and can decay.


A final manuscript is perfect in sense 2, not sense 1.

### Why the manuscript can be called perfect

- It fulfills its intended form. It says what it is supposed to say, with no errors, no omissions, no need for revision.

- Relative to the goal, it lacks nothing. The editing process is over.

- It perfectly embodies the abstract standard—the ideal text—that the author was aiming at.


But the manuscript itself is still:

- composite (letters, words, pages),

- temporal (it began, it exists now, it can be destroyed),

- dependent (on language, readers, materials),

- limited (it is a manuscript, not reality itself).


So its perfection is **borrowed**. It is perfect because it conforms to a timeless ideal. The ideal is perfect in the strongest sense. The physical manuscript is a temporal, imperfect copy that happens to match the ideal perfectly for its purpose.

### Analogy: a drawn circle

A mathematically perfect circle is timeless, partless, unchanging. A circle drawn on paper can be “perfect” for practical purposes—it looks exactly like a circle. But it is not mathematically perfect. It has thickness, irregularities, and it will fade. Its perfection is relative to the standard. The standard is absolute; the drawing is an approximation.

The manuscript is like the drawn circle. Its perfection is real, but it is a participation in a timeless perfection, not that perfection itself.

### So no contradiction

- **Absolute perfection** is timeless.

- **Relative perfection** can occur in time.


The manuscript is perfect in the relative sense. It is not perfect in the absolute sense. If you insist on using “perfect” only in the strongest sense, then the manuscript is not perfect. It is complete, flawless for its purpose, but not absolute.

That is how the final manuscript can be called perfect without violating the logic. “Perfect” is said in many ways. The manuscript’s perfection is one of them. The Absolute’s perfection is another. They are not the same, and there is no contradiction.
