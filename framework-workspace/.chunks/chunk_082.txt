The problem is the same one that haunts all ontological arguments.

1. **Definition does not entail instantiation.**

You can define a “self-existent unicorn” as a unicorn that exists by its own nature. The definition is coherent. But it does not follow that a self-existent unicorn exists. The concept may be empty.

2. **“Self-existent” already includes existence in its meaning.**

So to say “the self-existent Absolute exists” is tautological. It is true by definition. But tautologies do not prove that anything actually satisfies the definition. You still need to show that the concept is not empty.

3. **The gap is between essence and existence.**

You have described what the Absolute would be _if_ it existed. You have not shown that it does. Classical theism bridges this gap with additional arguments: the cosmological argument (contingent things require a necessary ground), the teleological argument, the argument from consciousness, or the ontological argument’s controversial premise that existence is a perfection.
