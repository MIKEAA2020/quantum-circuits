So you can prove:

That is a valid conditional. It is powerful. But it does not yet prove that a perfect being exists.

### The gap: from “best” to “exists”

“Perfection is the best” is either:

1. **Analytic:** If “perfection” means “the best,” then it is true by definition. But analytic truths do not entail existence. “A perfect circle is the best circle” does not prove a perfect circle exists. It only says: if one exists, it is best.

2. **Evaluative:** “Best” is a value judgment. Values do not automatically create facts. The best possible island is still just an idea unless something bridges value and existence.


To get from “best” to “exists,” you need a bridge premise. The classic bridge is:

> **Existence is a perfection. A being that exists is greater than one that does not.**

That is the ontological argument. Anselm, Descartes, Leibniz, and Gödel all used versions of it. But that premise is not a logical theorem. It is a metaphysical assumption. Kant famously rejected it: existence is not a property that can be added to a concept. A hundred real dollars do not contain more “being” than a hundred possible dollars; they just exist. The concept is the same.

So the argument is:

1. Perfection is the best.

2. Existence is a perfection (assumption).

3. Therefore, perfection exists.


Step 2 is the crux. Without it, the argument fails. With it, the argument is valid. But it is not assumption-free.

### The perfect island objection

Gaunilo replied to Anselm: by the same logic, a perfect island must exist. Anselm’s reply was that only God has necessary existence; islands are contingent. But that just shows the argument depends on a special definition of God as a necessary being. It does not follow from “perfection is best” alone.

### Necessary existence

You can strengthen the argument:

- A perfect being must have all perfections.

- Necessary existence is a perfection (it is greater to exist necessarily than contingently).

- Therefore, a perfect being exists necessarily.


This is the modal ontological argument. It is valid in modal logic S5. But it still rests on the premise that **necessary existence is a perfection** and that **a maximally great being is possible**. If it is possible, it exists. But the possibility premise is contested. So again, not a proof from definition alone.

### Within your framework

You can say:

- The Absolute is not a being that may or may not exist. It is existence itself, awareness itself.

- “Does it exist?” is a malformed question, because existence is not a property it has; it is what existence is.

- The Absolute is self-luminous, self-existent. It cannot lack existence, because existence is its nature.


That is coherent **within your framework**. But it is a framework-internal claim. It does not prove the framework to someone who does not already accept its axioms. It is a metaphysical commitment, not a neutral deduction.

### Honest conclusion

No, “perfection is the best” does not prove that perfection exists. It gives you a valid **conditional** and the core of a classical **ontological argument**. That argument is valid only if you accept the bridge premise that existence is a perfection or that necessary existence belongs to perfection. That premise is not a fact of logic; it is a metaphysical assumption.

So you have not proven the Absolute without assumptions. You have defined perfection, and from that definition you have derived what the Absolute must be like **if it exists**. To prove that it exists, you need one more step—and that step is the contested heart of the ontological argument. It is defensible, but it is not bulletproof. It is a metaphysical proof, not a logical one. That is the honest answer.
